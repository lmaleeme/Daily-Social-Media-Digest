US opposes Australia’s proposed social media restrictions

Australia's proposed Digital Duty of Care legislation would require social platforms to let users disable engagement-based algorithms, targeting addictive app features, AI chatbots, and content tied to eating disorders, crime, gender equality issues, and dangerous stunts. The U.S. embassy in Canberra submitted formal objections, as reported by BBC, arguing the bill's harm-content provisions could let Australia's communications minister unilaterally define and restrict "harmful" categories, effectively enabling localized censorship and government control over American tech businesses. Critics point to wording granting ministerial power to designate harm types, though the proposal does include parliamentary review and compliance checks against Australia's human rights and free expression obligations. U.S. officials are pushing for clearer definitions of harm and enforcement mechanisms, and have also raised concerns that independent journalists covering controversial topics could be caught up in restrictions. The pushback echoes conservative media campaigns against the bill, despite the legislation targeting content with documented harms rather than journalism specifically. The proposal remains in early discussion stages, with no finalized structure yet, meaning Australian officials still have significant flexibility to revise the bill's language and scope before any implementation.

Creator content preferred in AI recommendations: Here’s what the numbers say

The Interactive Advertising Bureau's "Creator Signal" report surveyed 2,200 recent AI-shopping users across the US, UK, Australia, Mexico and India in May 2026 and found 80% are comfortable relying on AI for purchase recommendations, with 56% preferring when those recommendations cite creator or influencer content. That preference splits sharply by generation: 65% of Gen Z and millennials want creator citations versus just 34% of boomers, with Gen X roughly split at 53%. Yet citing creators doesn't necessarily drive sales — only 29% of younger respondents and 12% of boomers said creator mentions actually increased purchase likelihood, with consumer ratings, subject-matter experts, retailers and brand content generally outranking influencers on that metric. Creators are seen as most useful early in the shopping journey, particularly for finding value/pricing (38%) and discovering new brands (32%), but far less helpful at checkout (15%) or final decisions (21%). Everyday consumers and professional reviewers are trusted far more than lifestyle influencers, journalists or celebrities, with credibility track record (49%) and consistency (40%) mattering more than follower count (21%).
