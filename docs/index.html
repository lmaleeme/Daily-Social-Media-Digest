Influencer marketing has an AI trust problem

Meltwater and YouGov surveyed consumers on generative AI acceptance across content types and found influencer marketing ranks among the least accepted uses, with just 28% approval compared to 47% for product advertising and 53% for entertainment content. The report also found 86% of respondents want content to disclose AI use, and 59% said undisclosed AI would damage their trust in a brand. This contrasts with earlier virtual influencers like Lil Miquela and Shudu, whose artificiality was openly part of their appeal; today's AI-generated creators in testimonials and haul videos are often indistinguishable from real people, raising deception concerns. Instagram has responded by replacing its "AI Creator" label with a more direct "AI-generated profile" tag and restricting distribution for synthetic accounts that don't clearly disclose their nature, after finding users resent discovering a seemingly human profile is actually AI. The research and platform changes point to the same conclusion: brands running influencer programs need stronger vetting of creators, centralized monitoring of content published under their name, and consistent upfront disclosure whenever generative AI is involved, since concealment rather than AI use itself is what erodes consumer trust.

Instagram tests read-only mode

App researcher Alessandro Paluzzi has surfaced evidence that Instagram is developing a read-only mode for viewing profiles, likely as part of the paid Instagram Plus subscription add-ons. When activated, this mode would prevent users from liking or commenting on posts while browsing someone's profile, and would also block messaging, following, and viewing that person's Stories. The feature hasn't been officially confirmed or launched by Instagram, and details on pricing or rollout timing remain unclear, but if added to the Plus package, it could make the subscription tier more appealing to users seeking additional privacy-oriented browsing tools.

Concerns about social media’s negative impact are growing

Pew Research's latest global study across 37 nations shows that negative views of social media's impact on democracy have increased in every country surveyed since 2022. The U.S. stands out with 64% of adults saying social media harms democracy, while wealthier nations overall show higher skepticism than developing ones. Despite this, 55% of all respondents still see social media as net-positive for democracy, with younger adults most likely to hold favorable views. A separate Pew survey found 82% of Australian adults support restricting social media access for minors, with older respondents most supportive of such bans, mirroring the broader negative sentiment trend. Similar majority support for teen social media restrictions appears in the U.S., U.K., France, Italy, Norway, and Sweden. The findings suggest engagement-driven algorithms that amplify divisive, emotionally charged content may be fueling public concern, and point toward growing political momentum for age-based social media restrictions as public opinion increasingly favors limiting youth access to these platforms.

Reddit ends support for RSS feeds

Reddit announced two developer tool changes aimed at protecting its data from automated scraping and bots. First, RSS feed support will end on November 13, 2026, since Reddit says RSS has become a major vector for large-scale scraping; moderators who rely on RSS for alerts are urged to migrate to Devvit apps before the deadline, though no alternative exists for non-moderator RSS use. Second, Reddit is folding its public data API into its Developer Platform, eliminating the previously available free, limited API access for approved developers and making all data access paid. These moves continue a years-long effort to monetize and restrict outside access to Reddit's data, following 2023 API price hikes, a 2024 Public Content Policy update, and lawsuits against scrapers allegedly supplying data to OpenAI and Meta. In Q2, Reddit's non-advertising revenue, largely driven by data licensing, hit $42 million, up 24% year-over-year. The changes also come as a PromptWatch report found Reddit citations in ChatGPT responses fell sharply last month, underscoring pressure to protect and formalize its AI data licensing deals.
