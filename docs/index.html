5 ways marketers can make a lasting impact with Gen Alpha

During Gen Alpha-focused sessions at Advertising Week New York, marketers from WildBrain, Whataburger, Church & Dwight and WPP Media's The Goat Agency shared strategies for reaching the under-16 demographic, projected by some forecasts to represent $5.46 trillion in value by 2029. DKC research shows Gen Alpha already wields over $100 billion in direct spending power and shapes 42% of household purchasing decisions, meaning brands treating them as a future-only audience risk missing immediate revenue. GWI and Razorfish data found more than 20% of Gen Alpha use AI daily, yet Whataburger's Donna Tuttle warned they resist being labeled an "AI generation" and instead want individuality recognized through authentic, hobby-specific content that performs well in AI search results. Church & Dwight's Channan Sawhney stressed optimizing content separately for human audiences and AI systems like Walmart's Sparky or Amazon's Alexa, leveraging influencers on Roblox and YouTube who shape both consumer behavior and AI training data. Panelists also urged brands to avoid shallow meme-chasing, pointing instead to licensing rising microdrama IP, and to revive co-viewing moments as cord-cutting fragments shared family screen time.

YouTube labels AI-generated music

YouTube has begun labeling AI-generated music across YouTube Music and its main app, tagging tracks its systems determine were primarily created using generative AI, including songs with AI-generated compositions and vocals. Tapping the new "AI" label opens a brief explainer confirming the content was AI-made. YouTube's labeling push reflects broader industry tension between enabling new AI-driven creative tools and protecting human artists from having their work and livelihoods undermined by synthetic content that competes directly with it.

Meta offers updated ad placement control guidance

Meta has been stripping away manual ad placement controls across its platforms, meaning advertisers can no longer exclude specific placements—like Facebook search results or in-stream Reels ads—for campaigns using engagement, leads, app promotion, or sales objectives. Awareness and traffic objective campaigns still retain placement control for now, but Meta signals this will likely extend company-wide eventually, prioritizing its AI-driven ad serving system over manual advertiser input. To ease the transition, Meta published guidance outlining four workarounds: customizing creative assets (images, videos, text, aspect ratios, URLs) per placement through Ads Manager's new multi-media ad workflow; using "placement value rules" to raise or lower bids for specific placements based on business value; maintaining account-level placement exclusions by objective through existing account controls; and partnering with creators or other businesses, which still allows control over media, messaging, and placement scope at the ad creation stage. The changes come amid pushback from advertisers worried about losing campaign precision, but Meta's broader trajectory points toward full automation of placement decisions, betting that AI-optimized distribution will outperform manually controlled targeting for most advertising objectives going forward.

Meta removes access verification for developers

Meta has removed the data access verification step it introduced in 2022 for developers whose apps tap into Meta business data on behalf of clients, meaning they can now go live without submitting business details for a separate review or waiting for approval. Previously, API endpoints checked whether a business was verified as a "Tech Provider" before processing requests, rejecting calls from unverified businesses as a safeguard against misuse. Meta says it can retire this layer because its Business Verification and App Review systems have matured and now rely on more sophisticated detection signals. The original 2022 policy was part of a broader effort to close data loopholes following major privacy failures, including the 2018 Cambridge Analytica scandal, where academic data access was exploited for political targeting, and a 2020 BBC report revealing 5,000 developers mistakenly got access to Facebook profile data. Removing this verification step marks a notable shift in Meta's data governance posture, coming as the company simultaneously pushes to expand business and developer adoption of its AI tools across its platforms.

LinkedIn exec offers insight into the battle against AI slop

LinkedIn is detailing how it's fighting AI-generated "slop" content, with VP of Engineering Tim Jurka explaining the system's mechanics in an Oct. 8 post. Since launching an in-stream AI slop reporting button in August, which surpassed 1 million uses within three weeks, LinkedIn has used that feedback to train detection models through a "teacher-student" setup, where larger models identify emerging slop patterns and generate training data for faster, smaller models. Combining these two methods, LinkedIn says its classifiers now cover all posts distributed beyond a user's immediate network with 94% precision in flagging AI slop, building on an earlier reported 40% drop in slop impressions. The update reflects growing platform-wide pressure to curb low-value, repetitive AI content as user skepticism toward social feeds continues to rise.
