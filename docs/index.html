Australia’s proposed algorithmic opt-out gets pushback

Australia is in preliminary stages of drafting legislation, the Digital Duty of Care, that would require social media platforms to let users permanently disable algorithmic recommendations and default to feeds showing only accounts they've chosen to follow. Communications Minister Annika Wells argued at the National Press Club that engagement-driven algorithms prioritize attachment over user wellbeing and that platforms currently make opt-out settings deliberately hard to find or maintain. The push cites research, including an August University of Texas study and a 2025 International Journal of Innovative Technologies in Social Science paper, linking algorithmic feeds to addictive behavior, depression, and dopamine disruption resembling substance addiction. Instagram chief Adam Mosseri has pushed back twice, including a recent Threads post, warning that removing recommendation algorithms would shrink Instagram's usage and reduce reach for creators as the "pie" of engagement gets smaller. Critics, including US commentator Michael Shellenberger at a Brisbane CPAC event, have alleged the bill could hand Australia's eSafety Commissioner excessive power over speech, including running fake accounts, though the article disputes those specific claims as misleading.

YouTube adds temporary pause for hacked accounts

YouTube has introduced an automated temporary pause feature designed to protect creators from account hijacking. When the platform detects unusual or unauthorized activity on a channel, it will immediately restrict several functions: uploading new content, starting livestreams, adding videos to playlists, creating custom thumbnails, and switching videos between private, unlisted, and public settings. This is meant to stop hackers from quickly locking out real owners and posting unwanted videos or streams. During the pause, YouTube's security team reviews the account, typically within 24 hours; if ownership is verified, full access returns immediately, and if hacking is confirmed, YouTube contacts the creator with recovery steps. Importantly, viewers can still watch and engage with existing channel content during the restriction, and monetization remains unaffected. Affected creators will see a notification banner inside YouTube Studio explaining the restriction, with a link to more details about the suspicious activity and guidance on recovering the account. The update adds a layer of security for creators who have invested years building audiences and revenue streams on the platform.
