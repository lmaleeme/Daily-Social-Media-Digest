Inside Omaha Steaks efforts to ditch marketing bull and attract Gen Z

Omaha Steaks debuted a "No Bull" marketing campaign in September aimed at winning over younger consumers by replacing vague terms like "gourmet" and "quality" with specific, transparent claims about its U.S.-sourced, hand-cut, aged beef. The campaign runs across TV, radio, YouTube, podcasts, paid social and influencer partnerships, featuring a 30-second spot that references the company's 109-year history while positioning it as modern and trustworthy. Within roughly two weeks of launch, Omaha Steaks reported a 46% increase in conversion rates and a 130% jump in social media mentions. The effort follows June's "America's Table" campaign, narrated by Kevin Costner, which tied the brand to major U.S. historical moments like the World Wars and the moon landing. Senior Director of Growth Marketing Angie Kubicek said the clearer messaging responds to consumer uncertainty around meat sourcing, especially since U.S. beef and pork distributors aren't required to disclose country-of-origin unless using a voluntary USDA label. The brand has also cut delivery times from over six days to about two since early 2025, with same-day delivery now available in nearly 50 markets, while still maintaining phone ordering and mail-based purchasing for legacy customers.

LinkedIn shares checklist for B2B marketing campaigns

LinkedIn has published a new checklist aimed at helping B2B marketers improve in-app campaign performance amid changing buyer research habits, particularly the growing use of LLMs to shortlist and compare vendors before sales conversations even begin. Because much of this evaluation now happens outside marketers' visibility, LinkedIn warns that measuring success only at the lead-capture stage overlooks most of the decision-making process. To address this, the platform outlined five core principles: conducting thorough market research to identify everyone involved in purchase decisions, broadening targeting beyond primary decision-makers, creating content tailored to each stage of the buying journey, measuring performance at every stage rather than just the final conversion, and using LinkedIn's tools, such as Conversion Tracking, to monitor post-ad actions. LinkedIn also recommends leveraging its audience targeting features to reach distinct buyer segments more effectively. The checklist consolidates these principles into a practical reference for marketers, intended to help brands align campaign strategy and measurement with how B2B buyers actually research and evaluate vendors today.

Meta discusses protocols for agentic commerce

Meta is developing a new open protocol designed to give businesses greater visibility and control over how its Muse AI agents interact with their websites, addressing growing concerns that led many sites to block AI crawlers over fears of security vulnerabilities, server overload, or agents exploiting limits humans couldn't (like refreshing a page hundreds of times per minute). Built in collaboration with Sierra and partners spanning retail, payments, and customer experience, the protocol will handle authentication and let companies see exactly what personal agents do when acting through their websites, APIs, or company-run bots. Meta says the system will be open for any business to implement, with the goal of making site owners comfortable enough to let AI agents transact on customers' behalf. The bigger challenge is that widespread agentic commerce could disrupt the entire web business model by weakening direct brand-customer relationships, eliminating upselling, and undermining display advertising if humans stop being the primary audience. Muse currently has over 3 million weekly users and more than 5 million downloads, underscoring the stakes as Meta pushes to expand adoption despite site owners' resistance.

Meta outlines political ad restrictions

Meta has announced its political and social issues ad restriction period ahead of the U.S. midterms, running from 12:01 AM PT on Tuesday, October 27, 2026, through Tuesday, November 3, 2026. Influencer marketing has an AI trust problem

Meltwater and YouGov surveyed consumers on generative AI acceptance across content types and found influencer marketing ranks among the least accepted uses, with just 28% approval compared to 47% for product advertising and 53% for entertainment content. The report also found 86% of respondents want content to disclose AI use, and 59% said undisclosed AI would damage their trust in a brand. This contrasts with earlier virtual influencers like Lil Miquela and Shudu, whose artificiality was openly part of their appeal; today's AI-generated creators in testimonials and haul videos are often indistinguishable from real people, raising deception concerns. Instagram has responded by replacing its "AI Creator" label with a more direct "AI-generated profile" tag and restricting distribution for synthetic accounts that don't clearly disclose their nature, after finding users resent discovering a seemingly human profile is actually AI. The research and platform changes point to the same conclusion: brands running influencer programs need stronger vetting of creators, centralized monitoring of content published under their name, and consistent upfront disclosure whenever generative AI is involved, since concealment rather than AI use itself is what erodes consumer trust.
