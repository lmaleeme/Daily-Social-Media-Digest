What marketers should know about the legal risks of algorithmic pricing

Marketers face mounting legal exposure around algorithmic pricing, where software sets different prices for different customers based on signals like browsing history, location, or loyalty status, rather than simple dynamic pricing that charges everyone the same fluctuating rate. New York Governor Kathy Hochul must decide by December 31 whether to sign the One Fair Price Act, a broad ban on individualized pricing across most industries, reflecting a surge of similar bills pending in more than 20 states. In December 2025, Consumer Reports found Instacart charging some shoppers up to 23% more for identical items; the company halted the practice, but New York Attorney General Letitia James still demanded its pricing records. A House Oversight committee has also sought records from Uber and Lyft, while the FTC has drafted guidance on when personalized pricing becomes deceptive or unfair. States vary widely, some banning the practice in grocery and retail, others requiring disclosures like New York's algorithm-pricing notices, with New Jersey allowing private lawsuits and treble damages. Attorney Laura Brett advises marketers to audit which data feeds pricing decisions, map where personalized pricing occurs, and involve legal counsel before deploying new pricing tools, since loyalty discounts carry lower risk than targeting by device or location.

Australia’s proposed algorithmic opt-out gets pushback

Australia is in preliminary stages of drafting legislation, the Digital Duty of Care, that would require social media platforms to let users permanently disable algorithmic recommendations and default to feeds showing only accounts they've chosen to follow. Communications Minister Annika Wells argued at the National Press Club that engagement-driven algorithms prioritize attachment over user wellbeing and that platforms currently make opt-out settings deliberately hard to find or maintain.
