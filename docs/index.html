Instagram chief shares algorithm insights and posting tips

Instagram head Adam Mosseri posted a series of videos on his own profile breaking down how the platform's content-ranking algorithm works, partly to respond to scrutiny like Australia's proposed regulations on algorithmic influence, and partly to help creators optimize their strategy. Mosseri explained that without ranking, feeds would be dominated by high-frequency posters like brands, reducing overall content variety and creator reach, so Instagram instead predicts engagement likelihood—such as watch time or shares—to sort posts, with recency still playing a major role in keeping feeds close to chronological order. He outlined four guiding principles: originality (favoring original creators over re-posters), recency, "breaking content" (testing small creators' public posts with non-followers to help new accounts gain traction), and growing model size (using broader data inputs to refine rankings). He noted users can shape their own feeds via tools like Favorites, "Not Interested," and Your Algorithm, which lets people edit assigned interest topics. For creators, Mosseri recommended focusing on engagement rate rather than total views or likes, experimenting frequently through trial Reels, and posting often to increase hit potential while balancing workload to prevent burnout.

LinkedIn shares insights into key B2B buying behaviors

Perhaps the most notable finding is that companies pairing LinkedIn Ads with consistent organic posting see 2.4 times higher win rates than those relying on paid campaigns alone. LinkedIn's takeaway for marketers: run longer campaigns, broaden job-role targeting beyond directors, and maintain organic activity alongside paid efforts for stronger results.

US opposes Australia’s proposed social media restrictions

Australia's proposed Digital Duty of Care legislation would require social platforms to let users disable engagement-based algorithms, targeting addictive app features, AI chatbots, and content tied to eating disorders, crime, gender equality issues, and dangerous stunts. The U.S. embassy in Canberra submitted formal objections, as reported by BBC, arguing the bill's harm-content provisions could let Australia's communications minister unilaterally define and restrict "harmful" categories, effectively enabling localized censorship and government control over American tech businesses. Critics point to wording granting ministerial power to designate harm types, though the proposal does include parliamentary review and compliance checks against Australia's human rights and free expression obligations. U.S. officials are pushing for clearer definitions of harm and enforcement mechanisms, and have also raised concerns that independent journalists covering controversial topics could be caught up in restrictions. The pushback echoes conservative media campaigns against the bill, despite the legislation targeting content with documented harms rather than journalism specifically. The proposal remains in early discussion stages, with no finalized structure yet, meaning Australian officials still have significant flexibility to revise the bill's language and scope before any implementation.
